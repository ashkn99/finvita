An ammonia spike in a tank that's been running for months usually means the bacteria colony that normally processes waste got disrupted — by overstocking, overfeeding, decaying matter, or filter media rinsed in tap water — rather than the tank never having cycled in the first place. Finding and removing the source matters more here than in a new tank, where the cause is simply insufficient bacteria.

Why did ammonia spike in my established fish tank?

In a tank that's been stable for a while, an ammonia spike is a disruption, not a starting condition. The most common causes are:

  • Overstocking or a recent addition of several new fish at once, which increases the bioload faster than the bacteria colony can scale up to match.
  • Overfeeding, or a dead fish or decaying plant matter hidden somewhere in the decor.
  • Filter media that lost its bacteria colony — commonly from rinsing it in tap water (the chlorine kills the bacteria) or replacing too much media at once instead of a little at a time.

Is this the same as new tank syndrome?

Not quite. New tank syndrome happens because a young tank hasn't built up enough bacteria yet — it's a normal, expected phase. An ammonia spike in an established tank means something disrupted a colony that was already working, which is a different problem to fix even though the immediate danger (toxic ammonia) is the same. If your tank is under 4-6 weeks old, see the new tank syndrome guide instead.

How to fix it

  1. Find and remove the source first — check thoroughly for a hidden dead fish, decaying plant matter, or a recent feeding change.
  2. Do partial water changes to bring ammonia down to a safe level while the bacteria colony recovers.
  3. Add a beneficial bacteria starter product to help the colony rebuild faster than it would on its own.
  4. Hold off on adding new fish or aggressively cleaning the filter until levels stabilize — both put more pressure on a colony that's already recovering.

How to avoid it happening again

Rinse filter media in removed tank water, never tap water — the chlorine in tap water kills the exact bacteria you're trying to preserve. Add new fish gradually rather than all at once, and keep an eye on feeding amounts as your fish grow, since bioload creeps up over time in ways that are easy to miss.
