A fish that's floating, sinking, or swimming sideways or upside down usually has swim bladder disorder — a problem with the internal organ that controls buoyancy. It's most often caused by overfeeding or constipation and frequently resolves within a few days of fasting, though it's worth ruling out a water quality issue too.

Will swim bladder disorder go away on its own?

Often, yes — if the underlying cause is diet-related (overfeeding, swallowed air, or constipation), a few days of fasting and a fiber-rich food afterward is usually enough to resolve it. Cases caused by infection or physical injury take longer and may need additional treatment; if there's no improvement after about a week, that's the point to look beyond diet.

What causes swim bladder disorder?

  • Overfeeding or swallowed air — especially common in goldfish and bettas eating floating pellets, where air gets swallowed along with the food.
  • Constipation from a diet that's too heavy on dry pellets and too light on fiber can put physical pressure on the swim bladder.
  • Infection or injury — less common, but a bacterial or parasitic infection, or physical trauma, can affect the swim bladder directly.

How to treat it

  1. Fast the fish for 24-48 hours to let its digestive system reset.
  2. Offer a skinned, cooked pea (a classic, genuinely effective remedy) or another fiber-rich food once you resume feeding.
  3. Soak dry pellets in tank water for a few minutes before feeding — this reduces the amount of air swallowed along with the food.
  4. Test your water if symptoms haven't improved within a few days, to rule out a quality issue as a contributing factor.

Can fish recover from swim bladder disease?

Most diet-related cases recover fully within days once fasting and dietary adjustments are in place. Fish don't typically need long-term intervention beyond that unless the cause turns out to be a persistent infection, in which case it's worth treating the water quality alongside the diet change since the two often compound each other.
