Cycling a new fish tank means growing a colony of beneficial bacteria that converts toxic ammonia (from fish waste) into nitrite, then into much less harmful nitrate. A fishless cycle typically takes 4-6 weeks on its own, or as little as 1-2 weeks with a bacteria starter product, and it's worth doing properly before adding fish.

How long does it take to cycle a fish tank?

Without any help, plan on 4-6 weeks for a new tank to build up enough nitrifying bacteria to safely process fish waste. A beneficial bacteria starter product can shorten that significantly — often to 1-2 weeks — by seeding the tank with the bacteria colony it would otherwise have to grow from scratch.

Can I add fish before the tank is fully cycled?

It's not recommended. Fish added to an uncycled tank are exposed to ammonia and nitrite spikes that can be fatal — this is exactly what's known as "new tank syndrome," and it's the single most common cause of fish loss in the first few weeks of a new aquarium. If you've already stocked and are seeing symptoms now, see our new tank syndrome guide for how to manage it from here.

How to cycle a tank step by step

  1. Add an ammonia source. Use a few flakes of fish food, a small amount of pure ammonia, or (less ideal but common) a few hardy fish to give the bacteria something to feed on.
  2. Test daily. Ammonia will rise first, then nitrite will rise as ammonia falls, then nitrate will rise as nitrite falls to zero. That full sequence is what "cycled" actually means.
  3. Speed it up with a bacteria starter. A beneficial bacteria starter product seeds the tank directly instead of waiting for bacteria to colonize on their own.
  4. Add fish gradually. Once ammonia and nitrite both consistently read zero, add fish a few at a time rather than fully stocking on day one — this avoids overwhelming a still-young bacteria colony.

Fishless vs. fish-in cycling

A fishless cycle (using pure ammonia or food to feed the bacteria) is the more humane and more forgiving approach, since there's no fish exposed to the ammonia and nitrite spikes while the colony establishes. A fish-in cycle — starting with a few very hardy fish — works but requires frequent water changes to keep those early fish safe from the toxic buildup, and isn't something to do with sensitive species.
