Holes or see-through patches in otherwise healthy-looking aquarium plant leaves usually mean a potassium or micronutrient deficiency, not pests or disease. A comprehensive liquid fertilizer that covers potassium and micronutrients — not just nitrogen and phosphorus — typically resolves it within a couple of weeks.

What causes holes in aquarium plant leaves?

Holes and thin, see-through patches happen when a plant runs low on potassium or micronutrients relative to how fast it's growing. It's especially common in tanks that dose nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizers but skip a comprehensive micronutrient supplement — the plant grows new tissue faster than it can properly build it, and the weak spots break down into holes.

Potassium deficiency has a recognizable pattern: small pinholes, often with yellow or brown edges, appearing on older leaves first. Potassium is a mobile nutrient, so when it runs short the plant pulls it out of older leaves to feed new growth, and the old leaves break down. If instead the newest leaves come in pale, twisted, or stunted, that points to an iron or micronutrient shortage rather than potassium.

Holes in Anubias and Java Fern leaves

Anubias and Java Fern are slow growers, so they're often left out of fertilizer routines entirely on the assumption they don't need it. They need far less than fast stem plants, but not nothing. In a tank with no fertilizer at all, these plants commonly show small pinholes or thin patches on older leaves — the same potassium pattern, just developing slowly over months.

Two other causes are specific to these plants. If the rhizome (the thick horizontal stem) is buried in the substrate, it can rot, and the leaves above it deteriorate and develop holes or brown patches. Anubias and Java Fern should be tied or glued to rock or wood with the rhizome exposed. And because their leaves live so long, old leaves eventually break down on their own — a single old leaf with holes on an otherwise healthy plant is normal.

Holes in Amazon Sword leaves

Amazon Swords and other rosette plants are heavy root feeders. They pull most of their nutrients through the substrate rather than the water column, so liquid fertilizer alone often isn't enough. Holes in sword leaves usually respond best to root tabs pushed into the substrate near the base of the plant, alongside regular liquid dosing.

Is it pests instead of a deficiency?

Usually not, but it's worth ruling out. Snails and some fish can nibble holes in soft-leaved plants, which tends to leave ragged, irregular bite marks concentrated on a few leaves. A deficiency instead produces more uniform, symmetric holes or thin patches spread across multiple leaves, often worse on older growth first. Tough-leaved plants like Anubias and Java Fern are rarely eaten by snails, so holes in those leaves are almost always nutritional.

A few fish are known plant-eaters — silver dollars and goldfish will happily strip soft plants. If you keep either, damage on soft-leaved plants is more likely to be grazing.

Can more light or CO2 cause holes?

Not directly, but they often trigger the deficiency. Brighter light or added CO2 makes plants grow faster, which raises how much potassium and micronutrients they need. A fertilizer routine that was enough before an upgrade can fall short afterward, and holes start appearing a few weeks later. If you recently upgraded your light or added CO2, increase your fertilizer dose to match. See plants not growing: light or CO2? for balancing the three.

How to fix holes in plant leaves

  1. Switch to or add a comprehensive liquid fertilizer that includes potassium and micronutrients, not just nitrogen and phosphorus.
  2. Dose consistently on a regular schedule — irregular dosing is the most common reason this comes back.
  3. Add root tabs for heavy root feeders like Amazon Swords and Cryptocorynes.
  4. Make sure Anubias and Java Fern rhizomes sit above the substrate, not buried in it.
  5. Trim off badly affected leaves; the holes themselves won't heal, but new growth should come in healthy once the deficiency is corrected.
  6. Expect visible improvement within about two weeks of consistent dosing (longer for slow growers like Anubias).
